    Methodology

    AI exposure Brookings Institution

    Brookings measures how much of the work in each county overlaps with what generative AI can do. It starts from occupation-level exposure ratings produced by OpenAI researchers (Eloundou et al., 2023), who scored the tasks in each occupation by whether a large language model could perform them or cut the time they take substantially. Brookings matched those ratings to 738 occupation codes in the BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS) and averaged them where several detailed titles share one code.

    For each county, Brookings multiplies employment in every occupation by that occupation's exposure rating and adds the results. That sum is AI-exposed jobs. It is a weighted count, not a headcount of individual workers, which is why it is not a whole number. AI exposure share is AI-exposed jobs divided by total jobs in the county. Across U.S. counties it runs from about 20% to 47%; exposure is highest in places with many office, professional, finance and tech jobs.

    Exposure is not a forecast of job losses. A highly exposed job may be automated in part, made more productive by AI tools, or both. The ratings reflect model capabilities as of 2023.

    Mark Muro, Shriya Methkupally and Molly Kinder, “The geography of generative AI’s workforce impacts will likely differ from those of previous technologies,” Brookings Institution, February 19, 2025.

    Labor market distress W.E. Upjohn Institute

    Upjohn measures distress with a single number: the prime-age employment rate, the share of civilians aged 25 to 54 who have a job. Focusing on this age group removes most of the effect of students and retirees, so the rate shows how well a local economy provides work for people who are generally expected to work. Unlike the unemployment rate, it also counts people who have given up looking for a job.

    The rates come from the Census Bureau’s American Community Survey, 2015–2019 five-year estimates, a period close to the peak of the last business cycle (ACS collection was disrupted in 2020). Upjohn sorts every place into five levels using the same cutoffs everywhere, set from the distribution of population across local labor markets:

    Sources

Employment by industry is from the BLS Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW), 2024 annual average (Connecticut, 2023).

    Industry mix

    Jobs are counted where people work, not where they live. Government is grouped by level, so public school teachers and public hospital staff count as government; active-duty military is excluded. "Not disclosed" is employment BLS withholds to protect individual employers. All jobs pools every job in the selection, so large counties dominate; Avg. county weights each county's mix equally. On the Largest industry map, stripes mark counties where withheld jobs exceed the lead of the largest sector over the second.
